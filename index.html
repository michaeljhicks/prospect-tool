01 / ICEWORKS BASICS
HEY THERE.

What is
Iceworks?

Iceworks is a locally owned commercial ice-machine leasing business. Instead of asking restaurants and other businesses to purchase an expensive ice machine outright, we provide the equipment and ongoing service through a monthly agreement.

We're looking for people who want to own a local Iceworks operation and build it gradually—potentially alongside an existing career, where employer obligations and the time required allow. It's business ownership, not a job offer or a promise of part-time earnings.

Why we're reaching out

Iceworks looks for prospective owners with relevant industry, sales, or business experience. If we've invited you personally, this is where we'll explain why your background caught our attention.

01 / WHY CUSTOMERS LEASE

Equipment comes with responsibilities.

Commercial ice machines and dishwashers can be expensive to purchase and complicated to maintain. Ice equipment also needs regular cleaning and sanitizing, and a malfunction can interrupt a busy kitchen. A monthly lease and service arrangement can simplify those responsibilities for the customer.

02 / WHY EXPERIENCE MATTERS

The hard part isn't only installing it.

There's an old medical-training analogy: learning the steps of a procedure is one thing; knowing what to do when something goes wrong takes years of experience. Commercial ice equipment is much the same. Iceworks has spent years developing its equipment sourcing, field-service relationships, software, and operating systems, so local owners have support when problems arise.

03 / WHY LOCAL OWNERSHIP

Local relationships matter.

Think of the difference between a company merely passing through town and a local owner who knows the neighborhood, answers the phone, and invests in relationships for the long term. Iceworks is built around locally invested owners, supported by local technicians and centralized Iceworks operations.

What's an FDD?

FDD stands for Franchise Disclosure Document. It's a legal document a franchisor gives prospective owners before they sign a franchise agreement or pay certain fees. It describes the business, required fees, estimated startup investment, contract obligations, and important risks. Think of it as the official information packet to review with your advisers before deciding whether to buy a franchise.

What does available funding mean?

It's the cash you say you could use for Iceworks, minus the cash you want to protect for personal expenses, plus financing that's already approved and genuinely available. For example, if you have $30,000 in cash, protect $20,000 as a personal reserve and have $15,000 in available financing, this figure would be $25,000.

Your salary, home value, retirement balance, and loans you're only considering do not automatically count as money available for the business.

What's a capital gap?

This compares your available business funding to the estimated cost of starting Iceworks, including any extra startup expenses you chose. A gap tells you roughly how much additional capital you may need to identify. It shows a low and high amount because the estimated startup cost is a range.

Example: if startup costs could be $60,000–$80,000 and you have $30,000 available, the gap would be $30,000–$50,000. A gap is not an approval, rejection, prediction of profits, or guaranteed financing amount.

    05 / FRANCHISE EDUCATIONTHE DOCUMENTS, THE FEES, THE QUESTIONS
    A SHORT GUIDE TO FRANCHISING

    What to understand
    before signing.

    The purpose of this page is to help you ask better questions—not to tell you whether you should purchase a franchise.

    01 / THE BUSINESS

    What does owning a franchise mean?

    You operate your own business while licensing an established company's brand and systems. You generally pay an initial franchise fee and ongoing fees, follow the franchise agreement, and remain responsible for funding and operating your business. Franchisor support does not eliminate business risk.

    02 / THE DOCUMENT

    What is an FDD?

    FDD stands for Franchise Disclosure Document. It's the formal disclosure document you receive before entering a franchise agreement. It describes fees, estimated startup costs, the franchisor's obligations, restrictions, franchisee information, and other important terms. Read it carefully and have your lawyer and accountant review it.

    03 / THE COSTS

    Which fees should I expect?

    The initial franchise fee is just one component of getting started. You should also review equipment and installation, available working capital, insurance, ongoing software charges, royalties, and any annual fees.

    Initial franchise fee—

    The initial franchise fee is already included in the estimated startup investment range—don't add it twice. The effective FDD and franchise agreement control; confirm current amounts and what's included.

    What do the fees mean?

    Initial franchise fee: generally a one-time payment for rights under the franchise agreement and the initial support described in your FDD. Royalty: the continuing fee based on gross revenue—not profit. Technology fee: a recurring charge associated with the technology systems specified in the franchise documents. Convention fee: a listed annual charge associated with the franchise convention. Ask exactly what each payment covers, when it's due, and whether other charges apply.

    04 / YOUR DUE DILIGENCE

    What should I investigate?

    Ask what equipment and expenses are included in the estimated startup range, what additional cash reserves you may need, whether financing is actually available, and what responsibilities you'll have for customers and service. Review territory terms, talk with existing franchise owners, and understand the conditions for renewal, transfer, or termination.
